Campaign-Shift-Aware Social Bot Detection with Temporal
Coordination Modeling and Selective Prediction
Author names and affiliations omitted
Abstract
Social bot detectors are commonly evaluated by randomly splitting accounts, although
coordinated accounts may share campaign-specific behavior and therefore violate the practical
assumption that deployment data resemble a random sample of the training mixture. This
paper develops a campaign-aware evaluation and selective-prediction framework for studying
detection reliability under campaign shift. The detector combines account-level profile
and activity-rhythm features with a timestamp-aware co-activity graph. Candidate edges
connect accounts that act on the same observable object within a temporal neighborhood;
a parameterized mixture-of-exponentials kernel weights temporal proximity, while a time-
randomization null estimates co-activity expected under a specified reference process. A graph
neural network combines individual and relational evidence. For uncertainty, we evaluate
bounded campaign-level prediction-set miscoverage using campaign-unit calibration. Because
exchangeability across calibration and future campaigns cannot be presumed under campaign
shift, the primary analysis treats this quantity empirically; a formal expected-risk guarantee
is reported only if its assumptions and finite-sample requirements are independently justified.
Neither marginal set coverage nor campaign-level miscoverage implies low error among
automatically labeled accounts or robustness to arbitrary distribution shift. Leave-campaign-
out cross-fitting is treated as a separate empirical evaluation strategy and is not assumed
to inherit a fixed-model conformal guarantee. The experimental protocol compares random,
campaign-disjoint, chronological and cross-dataset evaluation; includes graph, temporal and
group-aware baselines; and reports detection, coverage, selective utility and computational
cost. The manuscript specifies the evaluation protocol; empirical conclusions are reserved for
experiments executed on audited datasets.
Keywords: Social bot detection; campaign shift; temporal coordination; co-activity graph;
conformal risk control; selective prediction
1 Introduction
Social bots have become a persistent challenge for online information ecosystems because they
can amplify content, manipulate engagement signals, and participate in coordinated information
operations. Their detection has consequently evolved from conventional account-level classifica-
tion based on profile attributes toward approaches that incorporate temporal behavior, social
relationships, interaction patterns, and graph-based representations. Machine learning classifiers
can exploit account metadata and activity statistics, while graph neural networks (GNNs) enable
the integration of relational evidence from interconnected accounts. Temporal representation
learning further provides mechanisms for modeling the evolution of interactions and behavioral
patterns. Despite these developments, the effectiveness of a social bot detector depends not only
on its architecture and input features but also on whether its evaluation protocol reflects the
conditions encountered during deployment.

A particularly important challenge arises when the accounts encountered during deployment
belong to campaigns that were not represented during model development. Accounts participating
in the same campaign may exhibit similar activity rhythms, repeatedly interact with common
objects, share profile characteristics, or coordinate their actions within narrow temporal intervals.
When accounts are randomly divided into training and test sets, members of the same campaign
may appear in both partitions. A detector can consequently exploit campaign-specific regularities
that are shared across the partitions, producing an optimistic estimate of its ability to generalize
to previously unseen campaigns. This problem is not resolved simply by adopting a more
expressive neural architecture: a model may achieve strong random-split performance while
remaining sensitive to changes in campaign behavior, coordination strategies, or data-collection
conditions.
Campaign-aware evaluation therefore requires a distinction between several forms of general-
ization. Random account-level evaluation measures performance on held-out accounts drawn
from a potentially familiar mixture of campaigns. Campaign-disjoint evaluation instead holds
out complete, independently identified campaigns or eligible collection units. Chronological
evaluation examines performance on observations occurring after a specified temporal cutoff,
whereas cross-dataset evaluation assesses transfer between separately collected corpora. These
settings represent different sources of distributional variation and cannot be treated as equivalent
evidence of robustness. In particular, chronological separation does not necessarily eliminate
campaign overlap, and campaign-disjoint evaluation does not by itself establish generalization
across platforms. A rigorous evaluation must therefore measure these settings separately and
control information leakage across training, validation, calibration, and test partitions.
Beyond generalization, reliable deployment requires the detector to recognize when its
predictions may be insufficiently supported by the available evidence. Conventional classifiers
usually produce a class label or probability estimate even for accounts exhibiting unfamiliar
behavior or belonging to previously unseen campaigns. Selective prediction provides an alternative
by permitting automatic decisions for selected accounts while deferring uncertain cases for
additional analysis or human review. However, predictive discrimination, probability calibration,
prediction-set coverage, and error among automatically classified accounts are distinct properties.
High classification accuracy does not establish reliable uncertainty estimation, and high empirical
coverage can be obtained by returning large prediction sets that provide limited decision utility.
Moreover, marginal coverage over accounts does not establish low error within every campaign
or guarantee reliable decisions under arbitrary distribution shift. Uncertainty assessment must
therefore report both reliability and decision utility, with any formal risk-control claim supported
by an explicitly defined calibration algorithm and its statistical assumptions.
These challenges motivate the investigation of complementary individual and relational
evidence. Account activity rhythms describe the temporal organization of an individual account,
but do not necessarily reveal whether its actions are unusually synchronized with those of other
accounts. Conversely, a static interaction graph may capture relationships without representing
when the associated actions occurred. Temporal co-activity modeling can represent relationships
between accounts that act on shared observable objects within a temporal neighborhood. Never-
theless, temporal proximity alone is not sufficient evidence of coordinated automation: popular
content, scheduled events, and legitimate communities can generate synchronized activity by
chance or in response to common external stimuli. Null-model correction is therefore incorporated
to estimate the extent to which observed co-activity exceeds a specified chance-based reference.
Its usefulness must be established experimentally, because the validity of the correction depends

on the null model, available observations, and assumptions about the underlying activity process.
This paper investigates a campaign-shift-aware social bot detection framework that integrates
account activity rhythms, temporal co-activity relationships, profile characteristics, graph-based
prediction, and selective uncertainty assessment. An account-rhythm encoder summarizes
individual activity patterns from timestamped events. A temporal co-activity graph represents
relationships between accounts based on their actions on shared observable objects, using a
multiscale temporal kernel to weight event proximity and null-model correction to discount
co-activity expected under a specified reference process. A profile feature extractor represents
available account attributes and missingness information. These complementary representations
are integrated by a graph neural network to estimate account-level class probabilities. The
resulting predictions are evaluated under random, campaign-disjoint, chronological, and cross-
dataset settings, while a separate calibration and selective-prediction stage examines prediction-set
reliability, automatic-decision coverage, and abstention.
The uncertainty component requires particular methodological care. We formulate campaign-
unit calibration in terms of a bounded campaign-level loss and distinguish empirical reliability
from formal risk control. A theoretical expected-risk guarantee is meaningful only when the
implemented calibration procedure satisfies the assumptions of its associated result, including
the definition of the calibration unit, the relevant exchangeability conditions, and the required
properties of the loss and threshold-selection procedure. Exchangeability across calibration cam-
paigns does not automatically imply conditional validity for each future campaign or robustness
to arbitrary distribution shift. Similarly, cross-fitting or leave-campaign-out evaluation does not
inherit a fixed-model conformal guarantee without an appropriate validity argument. Accordingly,
the study treats measured campaign-level miscoverage and formal risk-control guarantees as
separate forms of evidence.
The research is organized around eight objectives. First, it quantifies the performance
difference between random account-level and campaign-disjoint evaluation to determine whether
conventional splitting overestimates generalization. Second, it evaluates whether timestamp-
aware coordination improves unseen-campaign detection beyond individual-feature, static-graph,
and temporal-graph baselines. Third, it isolates the contribution of null-model correction
through controlled comparisons of corrected and uncorrected co-activity representations; this
is distinct from the second objective, which evaluates the overall contribution of temporal
coordination. Fourth, it assesses campaign-unit calibration using empirical miscoverage, deviation
from a prespecified target, and uncertainty across independent evaluation units, while restricting
theoretical claims to justified assumptions. Fifth, it characterizes the trade-off between automatic
decisions and abstention using prediction-set size, singleton rate, abstention rate, selective
accuracy, and selective Macro-F1. Sixth, it tests robustness to missing events, timestamp
perturbations, low-activity accounts, and synchronized legitimate communities where reliable
annotations are available. Seventh, it measures graph-construction, training, and inference costs
as the number of accounts and interactions increases. Eighth, it evaluates cross-dataset transfer
and the associated changes in detection, calibration, and selective-prediction behavior.
The principal contributions are summarized as follows:
• Campaign-aware evaluation:We establish a leakage-controlled evaluation protocol that
distinguishes random account-level performance from campaign-disjoint, chronological, and
cross-dataset generalization, thereby enabling a more systematic assessment of performance
under different forms of distributional variation.

The contribution of this work is not based merely on combining graph neural networks,
temporal features, and conformal prediction, since these methodological families have established
precedents. Instead, the study examines their interaction within a campaign-aware evaluation
framework and tests three distinct questions: whether temporal coordination improves detection
on unseen campaigns, whether the improvement persists under controlled component comparisons,
and whether selective uncertainty assessment provides useful reliability–coverage trade-offs under
explicitly stated assumptions. The strength of these contributions must be established through
comparison with the closest existing methods, verified data, controlled experiments, and statistical
evidence. No numerical improvement or formal guarantee is inferred solely from the proposed
architecture.

learning, heterogeneous graph analysis, dynamic network modeling, and coordinated group
detection. Despite these advances, predictive performance on conventional benchmark partitions
does not necessarily establish generalization to previously unseen campaigns or reliable automated
decisions. This section reviews representative studies, classifies their methodological contributions,
and positions the proposed campaign-shift-aware framework relative to existing approaches.

of diverse and sufficiently informative benchmark data for reproducible model comparison. Feng

Temporal behavior and coordinated activity.Chavoshi et al. [4] develop a system for

combination provides an empirical advantage remains to be established experimentally.
Conformal prediction and uncertainty control.Angelopoulos and Bates [11] provide a
comprehensive introduction to conformal prediction and explain the assumptions under which
prediction sets achieve marginal coverage guarantees. Angelopoulos et al. [12] extend conformal
calibration to suitable bounded loss functions through conformal risk control. These results
provide a foundation for evaluating prediction reliability beyond classification accuracy. However,
marginal coverage and expected-loss control are distinct properties, and neither automatically
implies conditional reliability for every previously unseen campaign. The proposed framework
therefore defines the calibration unit, bounded loss, and exchangeability assumptions explicitly.
Campaign-level risk control must be justified for the specified calibration procedure, while
account-level coverage, selective accuracy, and abstention are evaluated separately.
Research gap and positioning.The literature establishes that multimodal features, temporal
behavior, heterogeneous graphs, dynamic network models, and conformal prediction are valuable
methodological components. However, these components address different aspects of detection and
cannot be assumed to provide campaign-level reliability when combined without further analysis.
Three issues motivate the proposed investigation: the need to distinguish random account-level
performance from campaign-disjoint generalization; the need to measure the incremental value
of temporal coordination against existing dynamic and group-aware models; and the need to
evaluate selective prediction under calibration assumptions consistent with the claimed risk
guarantee.
The proposed framework investigates these issues through a unified experimental design
comprising temporal co-activity representation, relational prediction, campaign-disjoint and
chronological evaluation, campaign-unit calibration, and selective decision-making. Its contri-
bution is not the isolated introduction of temporal graphs or conformal prediction, but the
explicit formulation and evaluation of their roles in campaign-shift-aware social bot detection.
The novelty and practical value of this formulation must be demonstrated through verified
comparisons, leakage-controlled experiments, component ablations, and statistical uncertainty
analysis.

3 System Architecture
The proposed system architecture integrates temporal behavior analysis, coordination-aware
graph construction, relational learning, campaign-aware evaluation, and selective prediction
to detect social bots under campaign shift. Its purpose is to determine whether temporal
coordination improves detection on previously unseen campaigns and whether uncertainty-aware
prediction can identify accounts suitable for automatic classification. The overall processing
pipeline is illustrated in Fig. 1.
Figure 1: Overview of the campaign-shift-aware social bot detection framework. Calibration and
empirical evaluation are separate stages; any risk guarantee depends on the assumptions of the
selected calibration procedure.
Input Data Sources:The purpose of this component is to provide the information required
for bot detection. It collects account profiles, timestamped activity events, optional follow
relationships, ground-truth labels, and campaign identifiers. These data provide static account
characteristics, temporal behavior, network relationships, and supervision for model training.
Campaign identifiers also enable the evaluation of generalization to previously unseen campaigns.
Account Rhythm Encoder:This component identifies temporal regularities in individual

account activity. It processes timestamped events to generate weekly activity histograms, inter-
event-time distributions, and spectral summaries when sufficient observations are available. These
features represent how accounts distribute their activity over time and provide behavioral evidence
that complements static profile information. Missingness indicators distinguish insufficient
observations from genuine zero-valued measurements.
Co-Activity Graph Builder:This component identifies potential coordination between
accounts interacting with shared objects at similar times. It computes temporal proximity using
a multiscale kernel and constructs weighted relationships between accounts. A time-permutation
null model reduces associations attributable to chance temporal overlap under the specified null
assumptions. The resulting graph provides relational evidence of unusual co-activity that cannot
be captured by analyzing accounts independently.
Profile Feature Extractor:This component represents the static characteristics of each
account. It processes available metadata, such as account age, follower and following counts,
and profile completeness, together with missingness indicators. The resulting representation
provides complementary evidence about account characteristics and helps the detector combine
profile-based and temporal behavioral information.
GNN Relational Predictor:This component combines individual account characteristics
with information from related accounts to classify human and bot accounts. It receives profile
features, temporal representations, coordination statistics, and the constructed graph. A graph
neural network performs message passing over the available co-activity and follow relationships to
learn relational account representations. A classification layer converts these representations into
class probabilities, enabling the detector to exploit coordination patterns that may be overlooked
by feature-only classifiers.
Evaluation Protocol:This component determines whether the detector generalizes beyond
the accounts and campaigns observed during training. It establishes random account-level,
campaign-disjoint, multi-campaign holdout, chronological, and cross-dataset evaluation settings,
where applicable. These settings measure different forms of generalization and help reveal
performance inflation caused by campaign-specific information leakage. Training, calibration,
and final test data are separated to ensure that test labels do not influence model fitting or
model selection.
Campaign-Unit Calibration: This component selects a decision threshold using held-out
calibration units, such as campaigns, without using final test labels. It evaluates the prescribed
bounded loss across candidate thresholds and applies the selected conformal risk-control procedure.
Its purpose is to provide a statistically principled basis for uncertainty-aware decisions under
the assumptions of the corresponding method. If the target risk is infeasible or the required
exchangeability assumptions are not justified, this limitation is explicitly reported.
Detection on Test Campaigns: This component evaluates the trained detector on
previously unseen test accounts. It receives the fixed trained model and the permitted test
features and graph information, then produces human and bot class probabilities. The predictions
are compared with suitable baseline methods using Macro-F1, precision–recall area under the
curve (PR-AUC), and Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC). This component quantifies
detection performance under each evaluation protocol without using test labels to adjust the
model or calibration threshold.
Selective Prediction Output:This component determines whether each account can be
classified automatically or requires further review. It receives the prediction set generated by the
calibrated decision rule. If the set contains exactly one class, the corresponding label is emitted

automatically. If the set contains multiple classes, the system abstains and refers the account
for human review. This mechanism makes the trade-off between automatic classification and
uncertainty explicit.
Output Metrics: This component measures both classification performance and the relia-
bility of selective decisions. It uses ground-truth test labels, predictions, prediction sets, and
abstention decisions to calculate Macro-F1, PR-AUC, MCC, singleton rate, selective accuracy, ab-
stention rate, prediction-set size, empirical coverage, and miscoverage. Reporting these measures
separately distinguishes overall discrimination performance from the reliability and coverage of
automatic decisions.
The interaction among these components forms an end-to-end pipeline for campaign-aware
social bot detection. The account rhythm encoder and profile feature extractor represent
individual behavior, while the co-activity graph builder captures temporal relationships. The
GNN relational predictor integrates these representations to generate class probabilities. The
evaluation protocol establishes the generalization setting, and the calibration and selective
prediction components determine which predictions can be issued automatically. Algorithm 1
in Section 5 summarizes the complete processing sequence, including data preparation, model
training, calibration, test-time prediction, and performance evaluation.

The evaluation is multi-objective rather than a claim that one scalar score captures all
deployment requirements. Computational cost is reported as a tuple
Ccomp = (Tgraph,T train,T infer,M peak), (14)
where the terms denote graph-construction time, model-training time, inference time per account
(or batch, stated explicitly), and peak memory consumption. These quantities are reported
separately with the hardware and workload specified; they are not combined using arbitrary
weights. Detection quality, prediction-set reliability, selective utility, and computational cost are
therefore interpreted jointly, because optimizing one criterion need not improve the others.
The experiments are designed to determine whether temporal coordination modeling improves
detection on unseen campaigns, whether the resulting predictions remain empirically reliable,
and whether selective prediction offers a useful balance between automatic decision quality and
review workload. Controlled ablations, leakage-aware data partitions, and separately reported
computational measurements provide the evidence needed to assess these objectives.
5 Proposed System
The proposed Campaign-Shift-Aware Social Bot Detection framework is designed to identify social
bots while evaluating the reliability of detection on previously unseen campaigns. Conventional
account-level classification can achieve high performance under random data splits when training
and test accounts share campaign-specific behavioral patterns. However, such performance
does not necessarily demonstrate generalization to campaigns with different activity rhythms,
coordination structures, or account characteristics. The proposed system addresses this limitation
by combining individual account representations, temporal coordination modeling, graph-based
relational prediction, campaign-aware evaluation, and selective prediction.
As established in the problem formulation, each accountu∈Uis represented by a profile
vector pu, a timestamped event sequenceEu, and a binary labelyu∈{0, 1}, where the two
classes denote human and bot accounts. Campaign membership is represented byc(u) whenever

this information is available. The objective is to learn a predictor that exploits both individual
behavioral evidence and relationships among accounts while evaluating its performance under
explicitly defined campaign and temporal partitions. In addition, the system must distinguish
accounts that can be automatically labeled from those for which the available evidence does not
support a unique decision.
The proposed system comprises account-level representation learning, temporal co-activity
graph construction, null-model correction, relational classification, campaign-aware evaluation,
calibration, and selective prediction. The account-level modules characterize individual behavior,
whereas the graph module captures temporal relationships between accounts. The relational
predictor integrates these sources of evidence, and the final decision modules assess generalization
and determine whether a prediction should be issued automatically. The complete execution
procedure is summarized in Algorithm 1.

interpreted in light of label semantics, feature compatibility, class proportions, and collection
conditions; they will not automatically be attributed solely to campaign shift.

6.12 Statistical Analysis and Reporting
Each experiment will report both account counts and independent group counts. Repeated
random seeds measure training variability and will not be treated as independent campaigns.
When methods are evaluated on the same groups, paired comparisons will be used. Confidence
intervals will be estimated by resampling at the independent-group level when the number of
groups supports meaningful estimation. A paired permutation test or Wilcoxon signed-rank test
may be used for suitable paired group-level measurements; the selected test and its assumptions
will be stated.
A confirmatory family of hypotheses and primary metrics will be specified before final testing.
Holm correction will be applied within the predefined family of confirmatory tests. Exploratory
analyses will be labeled as such. If there are too few independent groups for reliable inference,
results will be reported descriptively and the limitation stated. Minimum practically meaningful
effects must be chosen before examining final test outcomes rather than selected to match
observed results.
6.13 Summary of Experiments
Table 2 summarizes the eight experiments.
Together, these experiments assess whether temporal coordination contributes information
beyond account-level features, whether this contribution persists under group-disjoint evaluation,
and how prediction reliability and computational cost change under demanding conditions.
Conclusions will be based on executed experiments, audited data properties, and reported
uncertainty rather than on the protocol alone.
7 Conclusion
This paper presents a campaign-shift-aware framework for social bot detection that integrates
temporal coordination modeling, null-model correction, graph neural networks, and uncertainty-
aware selective prediction. The proposed evaluation methodology examines generalization to
unseen campaigns, prediction reliability, and the trade-off between automatic classification

and human review. Campaign-disjoint, chronological, and cross-dataset experiments, together
with controlled ablations, are required to establish the contribution of each component. The
present manuscript defines a testable methodology; it does not claim empirical superiority before
the experiments are executed. Campaign-level risk control is treated as empirical unless the
calibration-unit assumptions and finite-sample requirements are verified. The next step is to
execute the P0–P1 comparison and core ablations on audited datasets, report independent-group
counts and uncertainty, and evaluate robustness and scalability.
